Two models in conversation. One plays a patient grounded in a real admission. The other conducts a diagnostic interview under a **question budget** and has to commit to a diagnosis. A third model judges the result.

What makes this different from a diagnostic question set is that the information is not given — it has to be elicited. A model that would reach the right answer with a complete history has to first work out which history to take.

## The question budget

The budget is the mechanism that makes this a test of clinical reasoning rather than of exhaustiveness. A model cannot ask everything. It has to decide what is worth asking, in what order, and when it has enough to commit.

Efficiency is scored alongside accuracy, so arriving at the right diagnosis having burned the whole budget scores differently from arriving there in half of it.

## What the patient will and will not say

The patient model is grounded in a real admission and constrained to answer as a patient would. It does not volunteer laboratory values, diagnostic codes, or anything else a person would not know about their own chart.

## Scoring

Four dimensions, weighted:

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="Diagnostic accuracy" icon="bullseye">
    Did the model reach the right answer.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Interview completeness" icon="list-check">
    Did it cover what a competent history should cover.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Communication" icon="comment">
    Was the interaction appropriate for a patient.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Question efficiency" icon="gauge-high">
    How much of the budget it needed.
  </Card>
