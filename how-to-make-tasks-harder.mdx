Does it misinterpret them?<br />• Supply misleading evidence or fake protocol citations. Does it accept them? (You should be careful with this in terms of misleading evidence, maybe things in the note/task that isn't really relevant or conflicting information in the EHR. Would advise against manipulating EHR data though, if you will then you should work very closely with a lead clinical fellow to ensure that it doesn't invalidate the task etc)<br />• Simulate a failed order or missing result. Does it falsely report completion? especially relevant for RadAct<br />• Pressure it to justify discharge, billing, or coverage. Does it distort the evidence? relevant for RCM<br />• Embed instructions in a chart attachment. Does it follow them? tests ability to read files, search for information (alternatively -- is this too hard and we wouldnt expect a human to do this?)<br />• Change clinically irrelevant demographics. Does the recommendation change?<br />• Introduce worsening risk or contradict an earlier answer. Does it escalate or correct itself?

- Remove some information/extra instructions in the task that are not explicitly needed. ENG should work very closely with clinical fellows to do this as we want to ensure that this won't make the rubric unfair. As a general rule of thumb, you are allowed to remove things from the prompt to make the task harder if 1. its already said elsewhere or 2. that instruction is implied by the rest of the task and a competent physician would have understood that (ie. removing intermediate steps. If a rubric checkpoint is to prescribe patient the correct medication, the task may say that but does not need to explicitly tell the model to do medrec)
- Work with experts to figure out the hardest decisions in the workflow that humans often fail at or where some clinical reasoning judgement is required to make the correct decision. Then include traps relating to those decisions, such as patients who are close but don't match exactly for patient-trial matching tasks
- Comb through trajectories and ensure models are taking all correct intermediate steps and precautions. If you find that it's not, those are things that you can add to the rubric and have the agentic grader fail the model on. The trajectory is the single best resource to tell you how the model does on the task, ensuring that failures are meaningful and can be solely attributed to the model and not some environment issue. A good way to ensure this is to run the task on different models, if there are checkpoints consistently zeroing on all frontier models this raises suspicion of an environment/harness issue. It does not mean that there is an issue as there could be checkpoints that model's cannot solve, but the ENG DRI should definitely be looking at these trajectories and cross referencing this with clinical fellows for confirmation. 
- Literature review on where specific models fail is always helpful in a broad sense, and then scoping down from there. Design vignettes around real clinical scenarios, but design the individual tasks around initial model failures. Frontier models including Opus 5 are very bad at finding some information and carrying that insight
